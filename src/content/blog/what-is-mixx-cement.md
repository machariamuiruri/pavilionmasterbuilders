Most surfaces look their best on handover day. The paint is even, the grout is clean,
nothing has been dragged across the floor yet. Two or three years later the picture is
different — scuffed walls, tired grout, a bathroom that needs redoing rather than cleaning.

For a homeowner that is an irritation. For a hotel or commercial landlord it is a recurring
line item — rooms out of service, contractors on site, the same work booked again a few
years later.

That cycle is what Mixx Cement is designed to interrupt: a decorative micro-cement coating
now available in Kenya through Pavilion Master Builders, which launched it in Nairobi in
May 2026 alongside the South African manufacturer behind it.

## What Is Mixx Cement?

Mixx Cement is a thin-film, cement-based coating that goes over an existing surface to
create a seamless, polished-concrete-style finish. As a decorative cement finish it is
neither paint nor screed — it sits between the two. Thin enough to add almost no build-up,
but cement-based, so what you end up with behaves like a surface rather than a coat of
colour sitting on top of one.

The detail that tends to change people's thinking is what it will bond to. Mixx Cement
adheres directly onto:

- Existing tiles
- Glass
- Melamine
- Wood

That matters more than it first sounds. Refinishing a tiled bathroom or kitchen normally
starts with demolition — chipping tiles off, disposing of the rubble, making good the
substrate underneath, and only then beginning the actual finish. A micro-cement that bonds
over what is already there removes that entire stage. Less mess, less waste, and a
significantly shorter disruption for anyone who has to live or trade in the space while
work happens.

The result is a continuous surface with no grout lines and no seams — the concrete-look
wall finish that has become a fixture of contemporary interiors, achieved without pouring
any concrete.

## How It's Applied

Application is straightforward, and the tool determines the character of the finish.

**Roller** gives a smooth, even surface — the cleaner, more uniform look, and the usual
choice for large wall areas or anywhere you want the finish to recede quietly into the
background.

**Trowel** gives texture. Worked by hand, it produces the subtle movement and tonal
variation that reads as more architectural and more tactile — closer to polished plaster
than to a flat painted wall.

Either way, the coating is built up in thin coats rather than one thick application, then
finished with a **polyurethane seal**. The sealer is what turns a decorative finish into a
working surface: it takes the day-to-day abrasion, resists staining, and makes the surface
straightforward to wipe down. On a floor or a worktop, the sealer is doing most of the
hard work.

Because the film is thin, doors still close, skirtings still line up, and floor levels do
not shift — the practical problems that make people abandon a refinishing plan before
they start.

## Where It Works

The seamless nature of the finish is what makes it so portable between surfaces. In
practice it is used on:

- **Walls** — including feature walls, where the tonal variation of a trowelled finish does
  the work that would otherwise need cladding
- **Floors** — a genuinely seamless floor coating, with no grout lines to discolour
- **Kitchen and bathroom worktops** — a continuous surface with no joints to trap water
- **Wet areas** — showers and splashbacks, sealed against everyday moisture
- **Furniture** — melamine carcasses and timber pieces can be brought into the same finish
  as the room around them

Mixx comes in **separate interior and exterior variants**, which extends the same visual
language outdoors: facades, patios, and outdoor entertainment areas can carry the finish
used inside. For a project trying to hold one material story from the living room through
to the terrace, that continuity is difficult to achieve any other way.

There is also a designer colour range, so the finish is not restricted to grey — the
concrete look is a starting point, not the only destination.

## Why It Holds Up Better Than Paint or Tile

The durability argument comes down to how each finish fails.

**Paint** is a film sitting on a surface. It scuffs, it marks, and eventually it needs
recoating — and each recoat is a full redecoration cycle: furniture moved, rooms closed,
labour on site.

**Tile** is durable in itself, but a tiled surface is not one surface. It is many small
ones held together by grout, and grout is the weak point. It stains, it cracks, it
discolours, and it is far harder to clean than the tile it sits between. Most tiled areas
that look tired are actually grout that looks tired.

**Micro-cement** removes the failure mode rather than delaying it. There are no joints to
discolour because there are no joints, and the polyurethane seal takes the wear that would
otherwise reach the finish itself. Maintenance becomes cleaning rather than refinishing.

For commercial operators the interesting number is not the cost per square metre but the
interval between refurbishments. Hotels using the finish report going longer between
refits — and for a property where taking rooms out of service is the real expense, a
longer cycle is worth considerably more than a cheaper initial specification.

That is the case worth making to a developer or a facilities manager: not that it looks
better, but that it needs doing less often.
